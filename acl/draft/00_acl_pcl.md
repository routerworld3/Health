## Plain-language explanation

### 1. High partial ACL tear — 70–80%

The report says the **ACL has loss of fiber integrity involving 70–80% of fibers**. This means the main front-stability ligament of the knee is **badly partially torn**, not described as completely torn.

**What symptoms it can cause:** knee giving way, instability, difficulty turning/pivoting, fear of falling.

### 2. Partial PCL tear

The **PCL** is another major ligament, more involved with backward stability of the knee. The report says it has signs of a **partial tear**.

**What symptoms it can cause:** deep knee pain, instability, trouble going downstairs, weakness, swelling.

### 3. Lateral meniscus tear + small parameniscal cyst

The report says the **anterior horn of the lateral meniscus** has a **Grade III tear** and a small cyst near it.

The meniscus is the shock absorber cartilage. A Grade III signal usually means a true tear reaching the joint surface.

**What symptoms it can cause:** pain on one side of the knee, catching/clicking, swelling, pain with twisting, sometimes locking.

### 4. Osteoarthritis

The MRI shows **decreased medial joint space and bone spurs**, meaning arthritis/wear-and-tear, especially on the inner side of the knee.

**What symptoms it can cause:** stiffness, aching pain, swelling after activity, pain with stairs or walking.

### 5. Mild joint effusion

This means there is **mild extra fluid/swelling inside the knee**, usually from injury, arthritis irritation, or both.

### 6. Grade III chondromalacia patella + bone contusion

This means there is **moderate-to-severe cartilage damage under/around the kneecap**, plus a **bone bruise** at the lower part of the kneecap.

**What symptoms it can cause:** front knee pain, pain going up/down stairs, pain getting up from a chair, grinding sensation.

## What this means overall

This is **not a simple small knee sprain**. It is a combination of:

**Ligament injury + meniscus tear + arthritis/cartilage wear + swelling.**

Because the patient is listed as **70 years old**, treatment is usually based on symptoms and function, not MRI alone. Many people at this age are treated first with **non-surgical management**, unless the knee is locking, repeatedly giving out, or pain remains severe.

## Recommended next step

### 1. See an orthopedic knee specialist

Take the MRI report and images, not just the written report. The doctor needs to examine:

* How unstable the knee is
* Whether the ACL/PCL tears are clinically significant
* Whether the meniscus tear is causing locking/catching
* How much arthritis is contributing to pain

### 2. Start conservative treatment unless doctor finds urgent issue

Likely first-line treatment:

* Knee brace, especially if unstable
* Ice 15–20 minutes after walking/activity
* Elevation
* Avoid twisting, squatting, kneeling, running, and stairs when possible
* Cane or walker temporarily if balance is poor
* Physical therapy focused on quad, hamstring, hip strength, balance, and safe range of motion
* Pain control as advised by clinician: acetaminophen, topical diclofenac, or NSAIDs only if safe for kidney/stomach/blood pressure/heart history

### 3. Ask the orthopedic doctor these questions

Bring this list:

1. “Is the ACL tear functionally unstable, or can it be treated with brace and therapy?”
2. “Is the PCL tear mild enough for conservative treatment?”
3. “Is the meniscus tear causing mechanical locking?”
4. “How much of the pain is from arthritis versus the tear?”
5. “Would injection help — steroid, hyaluronic acid, or PRP?”
6. “Do I need surgery, or should we try 6–8 weeks of PT first?”

## When to seek urgent care

Get urgent medical evaluation if there is:

* Cannot bear weight
* Knee is locked and cannot bend/straighten
* Major swelling rapidly worsening
* Fever/red hot knee
* New calf swelling/pain, shortness of breath, or chest pain
* Repeated falls or knee giving way
